# The Map Bo French Doesn’t Want You to See (SE return 2026-10-27, accepted text)

Bo French made national headlines, and lost the votes of some leading Republicans, over a xenophobic social media post describing the number of students of South Asian heritage at last month’s Texas–Ohio State football game as a problem “far worse than anyone imagined.”

But the Republican nominee for a spot on the Railroad Commission of Texas, the state’s oil-and-gas regulator, has policy ideas too. He has vowed to roll back the agency’s recent “Green New Deal environmental regulations.” During June’s state GOP convention, he told a reporter that eliminating those rules was the very reason he’d chosen to run against incumbent Jim Wright, whom he defeated in a May primary runoff.

French cited one regulation in particular: that oil and gas producers—in most cases for the first time—must register with the state the locations of the waste pits that they dig and fill in the course of drilling and fracking. The same rules also require drilling and workover pits to be lined if the fluid in them is salty or the water table is within 50 feet of the pit floor, which the old rule did not.

The rules split the industry. Small independent producers objected most, and so did one large one. Last fall, Midland-based CrownQuest Operating sued to have the new rules declared invalid. CrownQuest’s chief executive is Tim Dunn, a billionaire and major right-wing political donor. Dunn is the main funder of the PAC that is French’s largest donor. In July, a Travis County judge dismissed one of CrownQuest’s two claims. The other is set for a hearing in December. Luke Dunn, Tim’s son and a CrownQuest vice president, wrote the Railroad commission that pit registration is “a significant recordkeeping and paperwork requirement with no benefit.”

Those who live near an oil waste pit might quibble with the notion that there’s no benefit to tighter regulations, even if hundreds of landowners argued that the new regulations were too lax. Waste pits are earthen holes, sometimes as big as several acres, that are filled with the byproducts of drilling. There are two main types. Inside workover pits you’ll find mud and rock cuttings—up to thousands of tons worth—pulled from the ground as a well is drilled. Meanwhile, produced-water pits hold the brine that rises up alongside the oil and gas, an estimated four times as much of the salty water for every barrel of oil. The cuttings can carry lead, cadmium, arsenic and chromium; the brine carries radium, which is radioactive. Both can carry PFAS, or forever chemicals.

Stand beside a workover pit and you’ll see a semisolid slurry of mud and bits of rock. A produced-water pit looks more like a sewage lagoon: an earthen basin inside a dirt berm, with plastic sheeting laid across the floor and up the berm to hold the brine in, and on most of them, the commission says, a second liner with a leak detector between the two. These toxic, radioactive ponds can breed bacteria that give off hydrogen sulfide, a gas that smells like rotten eggs. (At modern drilling sites, pits are increasingly replaced by storage tanks, with the byproducts trucked to hazardous waste landfills or injection wells, though that practice is far from universal.)

Under the commission’s rules a drilling pit must be closed within a year of the well being finished and a workover pit within four months, though in the ten years before the registry inspectors wrote up missed closure deadlines nearly 7,000 times, and nearly one in five pit write-ups ended in a referral for enforcement, a severance order or the state plugging the well. (The oldest case on the books is a pit in McMullen County written up in January 2018 and still open in the file in January 2024, when the state took the well over to plug it.) In the best cases, after they’ve outlived their usefulness, the workover pits typically are bulldozed to bury the waste solids, though sometimes the material is trucked off to landfills. Produced-water pits will ultimately have all of their liquid sucked out and transported to an injection well, to be placed deep underground. (Where it’s been shown to contribute to earthquakes and toxic geysers and “lakes” bubbling up to the surface.) But before it ends up in disposal wells, it often makes several trips from pit, to well and back again: under the 2025 rules, operators can store it in recycling pits and reuse it to drill and frack new wells; the state registry lists about 400 active produced-water recycling pits. The water in them is cleaned only enough to frack with: in 2022 the Texas Produced Water Consortium, created by the Legislature, said it knew of no large-scale operation in the Permian treating it further.

Many landowners and agricultural and environmental groups have warned that these pits pose a threat of contamination of the surrounding soil and the groundwater below.

Until last year, no one, the commission included, had a record of where most of those pits were. From the Railroad Commission, we obtained the records of every registered oilfield pit (as of August) in the state and mapped their nearly 7,000 locations.

Some of the registered pits are situated where they might affect water supplies. Where the state has a recent water-well reading within a mile of a pit, about three pits in ten sit over a water table within 50 feet of the pit floor, after correcting for the lie of the land—though for most of those the operator had claimed on its paperwork that the groundwater was further down than state data suggests. Another 112 pits are inside a FEMA-designated special flood hazard area, while state flood models and federal soil surveys mark another 87 in flood-prone sites. And 37 pits are within a half-mile of a well that feeds a public water system, and those systems collectively serve 185,000 people, mostly in Midland.

Those nearly 7,000 are likely a drop in a potentially toxic ocean. Pits closed before the registration requirement took effect in July 2025 generally aren’t in the registry, and the commission has more than a million wells on its books, many of which would have had a pit. Older pits closed in the year after the rule took effect, instead of being registered, aren’t in it either. One former waste-industry executive, Brad Zarin, told the Railroad Commission in 2023 that between 2010 and 2019, waste was “permanently buried onsite in over forty thousand pits … just in the Permian and Eagle Ford alone.”

Some in the oil and gas industry have asserted that there are no documented instances of waste pits polluting groundwater. But the state’s own case list says otherwise. The 2024 joint groundwater-contamination report lists 530 open Railroad Commission cases, fourteen of them filed under the name of a pit, including a reserve pit on an ExxonMobil property in Harris County and a disposal pit on a ranch in Jim Wells County. In each, contamination was confirmed by sampling and resampling, and most involve chloride, the salt in produced water. In the sixties, state investigators found that brine from unlined pits was the likely source of salt in wells near Coleto Creek, in Southeast Texas near Victoria, and in the Vealmoor oil field, north of Big Spring. The commission’s new regulations also require that, in cases where groundwater is less than 50 feet beneath the pit bottom, pits must have a liner. But the rule relies on an operator’s self-reporting. The registration form only requires listing an “expected depth to groundwater,” not—despite pleas from the Texas Farm Bureau—a measured one. One operator tried to persuade her that burying drilling waste was harmless, she told the commission: “it’s just ordinary mud, makes great fertilizer!” Most landowners, she wrote, have neither her training nor the money for a lawyer.

A record of what oil and gas producers leave behind is what French has promised to roll back if he beats Democrat Jon Rosenthal in November. (French did not respond to a request for an interview.) It’s not clear that it’s a move that his base—outside some in the oil business—wants, argued Brendan Steinhauser, a Republican political strategist. Texans are pro-oil, he said, but “it doesn’t mean necessarily that when they have something in their backyard, in their neighborhood, near their farm or ranch, that is an eyesore, or is polluting, or is making noise, that they’re not going to be opposed to that. I mean, they clearly are.”

One such conservative is Schuyler Wight, a rancher who has spent years pressing the commission over unplugged wells on his land. Campaign-finance records show he has given $7,500 this year to Rosenthal, French’s Democratic opponent. In a 2024 letter to the agency, Wight warned that “the industry is whining about the cost of increased regulation, but the Railroad Commission needs to consider the cost of polluted groundwater.”
